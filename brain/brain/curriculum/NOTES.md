# Spanish course notes (es-en)

The course ships as `es-en.json`, generated by `scripts/build_curriculum.py`.
Nothing in this file is hand-typed: vocabulary lives in the generator's SKELETON
table and the JSON is rebuilt from it. To regenerate after editing vocabulary:

```
cd D:/Projects/flylingo/brain
.venv/Scripts/python.exe scripts/build_curriculum.py
.venv/Scripts/python.exe scripts/build_curriculum.py --check   # fails if the file is stale
.venv/Scripts/python.exe -m pytest tests/test_curriculum.py
```

`--check` is the drift guard: it rebuilds the course in memory and compares the
bytes, so a hand edit to the JSON cannot survive unnoticed.

## Shape

Top level: `course`, `from`, `version`, `units`. Unit: `id`, `title`, `color`,
`order`, `lessons`. Lesson: `id`, `title`, `order`, `challenges`. Challenge:
`id`, `type`, `prompt`, `promptLang`, `answer`, `options`, `correctIndex`,
`audio`, `difficulty`.

Challenge types are `translate`, `select`, `match` and `fill`. `translate`,
`select` and `match` ask for a Spanish word or phrase and offer Spanish options.
`fill` shows a blank inside a Spanish phrase and carries the English meaning of
the completed phrase in parentheses, as in `Fill the blank: 'Buenos ___' (Good
morning)`. Options are stored in their fixed display order and `correctIndex`
points at the answer, so the client never re-orders them. `audio` always holds
the Spanish text to speak, which for `fill` is the missing word itself.
`difficulty` matches the unit number, so unit 1 is difficulty 1 and unit 4 is
difficulty 4: the scale grows with the course and never leaves 1 to 5.

## Vocabulary progression

Unit 1, Basics 1, difficulty 1, greetings and courtesy only. All 21 answers are
fixed phrases: no article, no number, no conjugated ser or estar answer appears,
which the tests pin against a list of forms (`soy`, `eres`, `estoy`, `haber`,
`habría`). The single place a later unit's vocabulary shows up early is the
u1l3c7 fill, `Me ___ Ana`, where the wrong options are `soy`, `eres` and
`estás`: they are ungrammatical inside that frame (the frame already says `Me`),
so the answer is unambiguous, and they target the beginner mistake of reaching
for "I am" instead of the fixed phrase.

- u1l1 Greetings: Hola, Buenos días, Buenas tardes, Buenas noches, Adiós, Hasta luego.
- u1l2 Courtesy: Por favor, Gracias, De nada, Perdón, Lo siento, Sí, No.
- u1l3 Introductions: Me llamo, Hasta pronto, Mucho gusto, ¿Cómo estás?, ¿Cómo te llamas?, Bien.

Unit 2, Basics 2, difficulty 2, everyday nouns and the articles that carry them.
Each noun is taught with its article, and the fill challenges drill el versus la.

- u2l1 People: el hombre, la mujer, el chico, la chica, el amigo, la amiga.
- u2l2 Everyday objects: el libro, la mesa, la silla, la puerta, la ventana, el teléfono.
- u2l3 Around town: la casa, el coche, la ciudad, el trabajo, la escuela, el parque.
- u2l4 Numbers: uno to seis, with siete, ocho and nueve kept in reserve for distractors.

Unit 3, Food and drink, difficulty 3, food nouns first, then the verbs comer and
beber as infinitives, then ready made cafe phrases.

- u3l1 Food: el pan, la manzana, el queso, el arroz, el pollo, la sopa.
- u3l2 Drinks: el agua, el café, el té, la leche, el zumo, el vino. Peninsular
  vocabulary is deliberate here: zumo, not jugo. `el agua` is the one noun whose
  article looks feminine, because the stressed `a` takes `el`; the u3l2 fill
  drills `___ leche` so the course never asks the learner to generalise `la` from
  the water noun.
- u3l3 Meals and verbs: comer, beber, el desayuno, la comida, la cena, el restaurante.
- u3l4 At the cafe: Quiero un café, ¿Tienes agua?, La cuenta, por favor, Está
  delicioso, Un vaso de agua, Me gusta el té. `Está delicioso` is taught as a
  fixed phrase, not as a conjugation drill.

Unit 4, Family and description, difficulty 4, family nouns, then possessives,
then ser and estar as separate forms, then adjectives.

- u4l1 Family: la madre, el padre, el hermano, la hermana, el hijo, la hija.
- u4l2 Family and possessives: el abuelo, la abuela, el tío, la tía, mi madre, tu hermano.
- u4l3 Ser and estar: soy, eres, es for identity and estoy, estás, está for state
  or place. Every prompt says which of the two jobs the verb is doing, so exactly
  one option fits.
- u4l4 Describing people: alto, bajo, guapo, simpático, inteligente, cansado, with
  triste, grande, pequeño, nuevo, viejo, moreno and rubio as distractor material.

## How challenges are built

Each lesson declares its vocabulary and its curated `select`, `match` and `fill`
challenges, plus a `size` between 6 and 8. `translate` challenges fill the
remainder, one per vocabulary item in lesson order, as many as the size leaves
room for. Because generated challenge ids are stable (`u1l1c1` and so on), the
option shuffle is seeded from the challenge id, which is what makes two runs byte
identical.

The size cap means the tail of a lesson's item list does not always get its own
`translate` challenge, and those items are then covered by the curated
challenges or serve as distractors: in u3l3 `la comida` and `el restaurante`
never carry a prompt of their own, and in u4l1 `el hijo` and `la hija` appear as
wrong options only. That is the intended shape, not a shortfall: every lesson
still teaches six or more items with at least one prompt each.

Distractors are picked by part of speech: lesson peers first, then the same unit,
then the whole course, and a candidate is skipped when its English meaning equals
the meaning the prompt asks for. That is what keeps options plausible: a question
about `la mujer` is offered `el hombre` and `la chica`, never a verb or a number.
Infinitives and conjugated verb forms are tagged separately (`verb_inf` against
`verb_fin`) so a prompt like "to drink" is never answered with `eres`. `tomar`
was removed from the pool for the reason given in the content audit below.

Every English prompt has exactly one defensible Spanish answer among the options
shown. Where Spanish itself is ambiguous, the prompt carries the needed context,
as in the ser and estar lesson and in "the friend (male)" against "the friend
(female)".

House style, enforced by the tests: peninsular neutral Spanish, correct accents
and inverted opening marks on questions, and no em dashes anywhere in the data or
the documentation.

## Content audit (2026-09-13)

All 97 challenges were read against the Spanish, not only against the schema.
Accents and inverted opening marks are correct throughout (`Adiós`, `Buenos
días`, `el teléfono`, `el café`, `simpático`, `¿Cómo estás?`, `¿Cómo te
llamas?`), every option is Spanish, and no prompt in unit 1 asks for anything
past a fixed phrase. Four defects were found and fixed in the generator; the
JSON was then rebuilt from it, so `--check` still passes:

- u1l2c6, match 'I am sorry': `Perdón` was one of the wrong options, but
  `perdón` also translates "sorry", so the prompt had two defensible answers.
  It is now `Gracias`, which cannot mean sorry.
- u3l3c2, translate 'to drink': the infinitive `tomar` was offered as a wrong
  option, and in Peninsular Spanish `tomar` does mean to have a drink. `tomar`
  was removed from the distractor pool and the u3l3 match set now offers `leer`
  instead, which keeps four options without a second defensible answer.
- u4l1c4, select: `Which word completes the pair, el padre y ___?` was also
  answerable with `el hijo`. It now reads `... the pair of parents, el padre y
  ___?`, which only `la madre` fits.
- u4l3c4, select: `Which verb do you use ...` asked for a verb but every option
  was a conjugated form. It now asks `Which form do you use ...`.

Two further points were checked and left alone on purpose. `Buenas noches` for
"Good evening" is the standard course pairing with `Buenas tardes` for "Good
afternoon", and the prompt carries no time of day that would make the two
interchangeable. `el padre y el hijo` can read naturally in Spanish, which is
why the u4l1c4 prompt names the two parents rather than changing the options.

## Validation

`tests/test_curriculum.py` runs 38 tests and passes. It pins the generator
byte for byte (`--check`), the counts above, the accents and the option order,
and it carries a mutation harness: 19 single-defect copies (wrong correctIndex,
duplicate option, too few options, answer missing from the options, difficulty 0
or 9, a type outside the four, promptLang other than en, empty audio, a missing
key, a lesson with too few or too many challenges, a non-hex colour, duplicate
lesson or unit ids, a dropped unit, a bloated total, an em dash, a non-Spanish
course) must each be rejected.

The validator is two layers and both are exercised: the pydantic models in
`brain/curriculum/__init__.py` catch everything that is a field or cross-field
rule (index, uniqueness, list length, difficulty range, type literal, answer
membership, id uniqueness), and `check_document` in the test suite catches the
document-level rules (6 to 8 challenges per lesson, 72 to 128 total, hex colour,
exactly four units, no em dash).

Two scripts outside the test suite repeat the work on demand, both read only and
safe to run at any time:

```
.venv/Scripts/python.exe scripts/prove_curriculum_validator.py   # 12 mutations, exit 1 on a miss
.venv/Scripts/python.exe scripts/audit_curriculum_content.py     # meaning collisions, English options, index drift
```

`prove_curriculum_validator.py` mutates an in-memory copy of the shipped JSON
and reports which layer rejected each defect; its last run caught 12 of 12 with
no misses, including all five required cases (wrong `correctIndex`, duplicate
option, only two options, `difficulty` 9, a bogus `type`). The semantic audit
reports 97 challenges, 0 options carrying the prompt's meaning, 0 options in
English and 0 index problems; its one standing observation is that two option
sets mix parts of speech (`u1l3c7` pairs the answer `llamo` with the ser and
estar forms, `u4l2c4` pairs the possessive `mi madre` with bare family nouns),
which does not create a second defensible answer in either case.
